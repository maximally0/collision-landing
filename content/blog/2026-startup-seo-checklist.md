Most SEO checklists circulating online have fifty items and no prioritization, which for an early-stage startup with genuinely limited time is arguably worse than no checklist at all — it makes everything feel equally urgent when almost none of it is. Here's a shorter, explicitly ordered version, grounded in what Google's own documentation says it actually rewards, not in generic "best practices" folklore.

## 1. Make sure the basics actually exist

This sounds too obvious to include, and it's the single most common issue we find when auditing real startup sites: no `robots.txt`, no `sitemap.xml`, a broken Open Graph image reference that's been silently killing every social share preview for months without anyone noticing. None of this requires strategy or creativity — it requires actually checking that the files exist and that every URL they reference resolves to something real. We found exactly this class of bug on our own site during a recent audit, which is part of why it's first on this list.

## 2. Fix your metadata before you write a single new post

A precise, specific `<title>` tag and meta description on every page beats another blog post nobody finds, because metadata is the thing that determines whether someone clicks through from a results page at all. If your homepage title still says something generic, or your meta description is boilerplate that could describe any company in your category, that's a five-minute fix with an outsized return relative to the effort — fix this before investing in new content.

## 3. Add structured data where it's actually true

`Organization` schema for your company, `SoftwareApplication` or `Product` schema for what you sell, `FAQPage` schema if you have genuine FAQ content on the page. Structured data doesn't move rankings directly on its own in any way Google has confirmed, but it materially improves how you can show up in results — rich snippets, knowledge panel eligibility — and increasingly affects how accurately AI answer engines describe you when someone asks about your category. The one hard rule: never add schema describing something that isn't visibly true on the page. Google's guidance is explicit that mismatched structured data gets discounted, and it's a reasonable trust violation either way.

## 4. One clear H1 per page, logical heading structure underneath it

Every page should have exactly one `<h1>` that states plainly what the page is about, with `<h2>`s and `<h3>`s nesting logically underneath to break the content into scannable, specifically-titled sections. This is a five-minute audit, and a genuinely surprising number of startup sites fail it — often because a page has zero `<h1>` tags, or several competing ones.

## 5. Publish content with a real point of view, on a cadence you can actually sustain

This is the slow-compounding item on this list, and it's where most startups either give up too early or burn out attempting an unsustainable pace. Google's own content guidance is explicit and worth quoting directly: it lists "writing to a particular word count because you've heard Google has a preferred word count" as a warning sign of the wrong approach, and states plainly that Google doesn't have one. What the guidance does reward is demonstrated first-hand expertise, depth on a specific topic, and content that leaves a reader genuinely satisfied rather than needing to search again elsewhere for a better answer.

In practice, this means: better to publish one genuinely specific, useful post every two weeks — grounded in something you actually know because you've lived it — than five generic ones in a burst sprint followed by six months of silence. Both search engines and the newer AI answer engines reward the same underlying signal: consistency and specificity, not volume.

## 6. Track qualified traffic, not impressions

Impressions are trivially easy to inflate through low-relevance content and mean very little on their own. What actually matters is whether the people finding you are the people you're trying to reach in the first place — and that requires actually looking at who's showing up and what they do next, not just how many of them arrived.

## What to deliberately skip, for now

Aggressive backlink outreach campaigns, keyword-stuffing low-value pages, and reactively chasing every algorithm-update headline that shows up in your feed are all lower priority than the six items above for an early-stage company with limited time. Get the fundamentals — the files that should exist, the metadata that should be specific, the structured data that should be true, the headings that should be logical, the content that should be genuinely useful — solidly right first. The advanced tactics only compound once that foundation is actually solid; applied before that, they're mostly wasted effort on top of a leaky base.
