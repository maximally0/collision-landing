Most LinkedIn advice stops at "post consistently." That advice is necessary but nowhere near sufficient — plenty of founders post every single day for a year and go essentially nowhere, while others build meaningful reach posting a fraction as often. The difference isn't frequency. Here's what actually separates the accounts that compound from the ones that plateau.

## Specificity beats frequency, every time

The platform's distribution mechanics reward engagement, and engagement follows specificity, not volume. "5 tips for better productivity" gets skimmed for three seconds and ignored — the reader has seen a thousand versions of this post and their pattern-matching correctly tells them there's nothing new here. "We lost our biggest customer because of a specific decision I made in week three, and here's exactly what I got wrong" gets read to the end, because it's a claim that could only come from one specific person's specific experience, and that specificity is what makes it worth the reader's attention.

Founders with genuine reach on the platform almost always write from a specific, sometimes uncomfortable, first-person vantage point — a real decision, a real number, a real mistake — rather than generic advice content that could have been written by anyone about anything.

## The comment section is not optional — it's half the job

Posting is the visible half of building on LinkedIn. Replying — to comments on your own posts, and thoughtfully to other people's posts in your specific space — is the other half, and it's the half most founders skip because it doesn't feel like "real" content work. It is real work, and it's disproportionately where early reach actually comes from: a thoughtful reply on someone else's post puts your name and thinking in front of that person's audience, which is a fundamentally different and often more efficient distribution mechanism than posting into your own smaller network and hoping it spreads.

## Consistency compounds; a single viral hit mostly doesn't

A single viral post gives you a short-lived spike and a lot of low-intent followers who churn out of your feed within a week or two, because they followed the moment, not the person. A steady cadence of specific, on-topic posts over months builds an audience that actually recognizes your name and remembers what you think about — and that recognized audience is the one that eventually turns into inbound interest, replies to your DMs, and introductions to people who need what you're building. Optimize for the second outcome; the first one is fun but mostly doesn't compound.

## What "good LinkedIn" actually requires operationally

For a founder without a dedicated content person, the real constraint usually isn't strategy — most founders who've been paying attention already have a rough sense of what "good LinkedIn" looks like, even if they haven't formalized it. The real constraint is throughput: can you consistently turn what's actually happening in the business this week — a hard decision, a customer conversation that surprised you, a mistake you're willing to be honest about, a small but real win — into a specific, first-person post several times a week, and reply to what it generates, without it consuming your entire afternoon?

## Why the constraint is execution, not ideas

This is worth stating plainly because it changes what actually helps: founders rarely fail at LinkedIn because they lack anything worth saying. The business itself generates specific, postable material constantly — decisions, mistakes, small wins, customer conversations. What breaks is the pipeline from "something specific just happened" to "a post that captures it, in your voice, published, with replies handled" happening consistently enough to compound, week after week, without eating the hours the founder needs for the actual company.
